A chronicle in thirty verses

The Book of the Sojourners

An empire spent four hundred years in other people's countries. Then it drew a line, went home, and asked why the people followed. Follow the line down.

Scroll

I  ·  1600 – 1858  ·  The Company

They came to buy, and stayed to rule

1Now there came unto the land of the five rivers and the great delta a company of merchants out of a small isle in the cold sea, and they were few, and they said, We seek only a place to lay down our cloth and take up thine.

2And the princes of the land said, Let them come, for they are few. And the merchants raised a fort, and about the fort a wall, and within the wall an army, and the army was greater than the trade. The East India Company's private army numbered roughly 260,000 by 1803 — about twice the size of the British Army itself.

3In the space of an hundred years the company that came to buy became the company that ruled, and the land was written into a ledger, and the ledger was carried across the water.

4And the looms of Bengal, which had clothed the nations, fell silent; and the cotton went out raw and returned as cloth, and the price of the returning was set in the isle. And the weavers went into the fields, and the fields were sown with indigo and with poppy and with what the isle desired, and not with grain. India's share of world manufacturing output fell from roughly 25% in 1750 to about 2% by 1900 (Bairoch's estimates). Bengal's indigo cultivators revolted in 1859.

5Then came the years when the rain failed. And there was corn in the granaries and ships in the harbour, and the ships were laden, and they sailed. And the people ate the seed, and after the seed they ate nothing, and their bones were counted in the ledger under the heading of unfortunate seasons.

II  ·  1914 – 1919  ·  The blood tax

Send us thy sons

6And it came to pass in the days of the great wars of the north, that the isle sent word to the land, saying, Send us thy sons.

7And the sons went — a million and a half, and after them two millions more — unto the mud of Flanders and the sands of Africa and the jungles of the east, and they died for a king they had never seen, in a cause that was not theirs, in a freedom they were not given. About 1.5 million Indians served in the First World War; some 2.5 million volunteered in the Second — the largest volunteer army ever raised.

8And the survivors returned, and said one to another, We have bled for their liberty; where then is ours?

9And they gathered, and there was a garden in Amritsar, and it had one gate. 13 April 1919. Jallianwala Bagh. 1,650 rounds fired into an enclosed garden. Official count: 379 dead. The Congress inquiry put it above a thousand.

Selah.

III  ·  1947  ·  The award

A barrister, a map, and a pencil

10And after long striving the isle grew weary, for its treasury was spent and its own cities were rubble, and it said, Take back thy land.

11And a barrister came out of the isle, and he had forty days, and he had never set foot upon that ground, and he was given a map and a pencil. Cyril Radcliffe landed on 8 July 1947 and had roughly five weeks. He had never been to India. He worked from outdated maps and census returns.

12And he drew a line through villages and through fields and through the courtyards of houses, and he did not know what he had drawn, and he burned his papers and went home and would not speak of it. The award was withheld until 17 August — two days after independence. Radcliffe destroyed his working papers and refused his fee.

13And upon that line fifteen millions walked, going each to the country that had been made for them. Estimates of displacement run from 12 to 20 million people — the largest mass migration in recorded history.

14And the trains came into the stations, and the trains were full, and no man cried out from within them, for there was none living to cry.

15And a million lay down in the fields between the two flags, and the flags were new, and the rivers were red, and the land was free. Death tolls are contested: commonly cited between 200,000 and 2 million. No authority ever counted them.

IV  ·  1948 – 1967  ·  The invitation

It held out its hand

16Now in those days the isle across the water lay broken, and there were not hands enough to raise it. Its mills stood idle, its buses stood still, and the sick had none to tend them.

17And the isle wrote a law, and said: whosoever is of the Commonwealth is a subject of the Crown, and may come and dwell and labour among us. The British Nationality Act 1948 gave some 800 million Commonwealth subjects the right to enter and settle in Britain.

18For it had held them by the collar for two centuries, and now it held out its hand.

19And they came. From Sylhet and from Mirpur, from Punjab and Gujarat, they came unto Bradford and unto Southall and unto Whitechapel, and they took up the night shifts that no man of the isle would take. Textile mills ran night shifts staffed almost entirely by South Asian labour. The NHS recruited nurses directly from the Commonwealth from its founding year.

20And they were as Ruth, who said unto Naomi, whither thou goest, I will go — for Ruth followed her mother-in-law home, and the home was not hers, and she gleaned in the corners of another man's field.

21And Mirpur was drowned beneath a dam, and its people were scattered, and the engineers who drowned it were of the isle; and so they followed the water to its source. The Mangla Dam, completed 1967, was British-engineered and part-funded. It displaced around 110,000 people in Mirpur. Compensation included work vouchers for Britain. Mirpuris remain a large share of British Pakistanis.

V  ·  1962 – 1981  ·  The door

Shut behind the labourer

22And the men of the isle stood in their doorways and said: Why are ye come into our land? Why do ye trouble our streets with thy speech and thy cooking-smoke? Get thee back unto thine own country, for it is a poor country. Enoch Powell gave the "rivers of blood" speech on 20 April 1968. As Minister of Health from 1960 he had personally overseen the recruitment of Commonwealth doctors and nurses to staff the NHS.

23And the sojourner answered and said: My country is poor because thou wast in it. The loom thou brakest, the grain thou tookest, the line thou drewest, the debt thou leftest. I did not come unto thy house uninvited; I have come home with thee.

25And the men of the isle were sore afraid, and made laws, and each law was narrower than the last, until the door that had been opened for the labour was shut behind the labourer.

VI  ·  Now  ·  The stranger in the gates

The reversal you did not expect

26But it is written: Thou shalt not oppress a stranger, for ye know the heart of a stranger, seeing ye were strangers in the land of Egypt. Exodus 23:9, King James Version.

27And this is the reversal, and it is not the one thou didst expect: it is not the sojourner who was in Egypt. It is the house he came to. For that house went out into all the earth and dwelt in the lands of others, uninvited, four hundred years, and called it civilisation.

28And now the stranger is in its gates, and it knows not the heart of a stranger, though it wrote the book of it in blood.

29And the children of the sojourners were born in the isle, and they spoke its tongue as their own, and were told: go home.

30And they said: this is home. And that word was the last conquest, and it was not won by an army.

On the record

The verses are written in the register of the King James Bible — the empire's own scripture, in the empire's own English. The type is Libre Caslon, after the letterform the East India Company printed its proclamations in. The marginal notes are set in typescript, as a clerk would enter them.

Figures in the margins are drawn from standard historical estimates and are contested where noted. Casualty counts for the Bengal famines and for Partition have no agreed authority; the ranges given are the ones historians argue between.

The page opens in the green of an indigo vat and settles into blue, as dyed cloth does when it meets the air.
