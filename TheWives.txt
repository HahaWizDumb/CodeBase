# What the Ark Remembered
 
Nahara was too old to carry water, so they gave her the children instead.
 
She sat them in the shade of the tamarisk in threes, always threes, because three is what the mouth can hold. "Say it back to me," she said. "Shem. Ham. Japheth. Say it until it is a stone in your pocket."
 
They said it. They said it while they picked stones from lentils, they said it walking to the well, they said it half-asleep, and that was the point. A thing you can say without thinking is a thing that outlives the one who taught it. Nahara had never written a word in her life. She did not consider this a poverty. Writing rots, burns, gets buried under a city. A song walks.
 
"Now," she said. "Before the water."
 
---
 
Before the water, she told them, the world was not one people. It was many.
 
There were the people of the cold stone, who lived where the year had teeth. They were shorter than us and heavier, and their eyes were large because they lived under a low grey sky and needed to gather what light there was. They buried their dead with flowers. They kept fire the way a mother keeps a sleeping child. They had no cities and did not want any, and when they spoke it was low in the chest, so that at a distance you would think it was the hill itself talking.
 
There were the people of the high east, past the rivers, past the passes where the air is thin enough to make a man weep for no reason. Almost nobody saw them. They were the ones who could breathe where breath ran out. They left almost nothing behind — a tooth, a finger-bone, a story — and this is why they are the hardest to say, and why the tellers stopped saying them, and why they nearly went out of the world entirely.
 
And there were the people of the long grass, who came up out of the south and went everywhere. Thin, restless, quick to argue and quick to draw. They talked too much. They are us.
 
"Which one were we?" asked the boy with the crooked tooth, because there is always one who asks it that way.
 
Nahara took his chin in her hand. "Wrong question," she said. "That is the question the water was sent to drown."
 
---
 
Here is what happened, she told them, and here is why it is told the way it is told.
 
The world grew loud and cruel and the sky opened, and the ark was built, and the ark carried three sons and their wives. That is the part everyone remembers, because it is short and it has animals in it and children will repeat anything with animals in it. But the part underneath — the part you must hold in your mouth like a stone —
 
The wives.
 
Nobody remembers their names. That is not an accident. That is a hiding place.
 
Because the sons were of the long grass, all three of them, the restless arguing kind. But the wives were not. One came from the cold stone, and one from the high east, and one from the grass, and when the water went down and the sons went out to fill the earth, they did not go out alone. They went out carrying what they had married.
 
Japheth went north into the mist and the isles, and the cold-stone blood in his house made his children broad and pale and patient with winter, and they kept fire the way their mother's mother had kept it.
 
Shem stayed between the rivers and made the words that we are speaking now.
 
Ham went south and east, and his house went further than anyone — over water, to islands nobody has counted — and in the far ones, the high-east blood woke up again and let them climb where the air runs out.
 
"So the other kinds died," said the girl who never spoke.
 
"No," said Nahara. "Listen. That is the second question the water was sent to drown."
 
They did not die. They were *married*. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole story. The cold-stone people are not gone; they are in the shape of your brow and the set of your teeth and the way your body burns a fever. The high-east people are not gone; they are in the lungs of men living on mountains they have never seen a map of. Nothing that was ever loved got thrown overboard.
 
The ark is not a boat. The ark is you.
 
---
 
"That's not how it's told," said the boy with the crooked tooth. "In the market they tell it with the animals and the raven and the dove."
 
"Good," said Nahara. "Let them. The animals are the handles. You do not carry a jar by the water."
 
She made them say it again — Shem, Ham, Japheth, north, between, south — and she made them say it in the rhythm that walks, the one you can keep up for a day's travel, the one a grandmother can teach a grandchild in an afternoon and that grandchild can teach hers, on and on, down a chain longer than any king's list, longer than any city's life.
 
"It will get worn," she admitted. "Every time it is said it is said a little smoother. In a thousand years they will think the sons were nations and the wives were nobody and the water was a season of hard rain. Fine. Let it wear. A stone in a river still stops a river."
 
Then she said the thing they remembered longest, and repeated wrong, and repeated wrong again, until it came down to us as a line about begetting:
 
"Every one of you is a crowd. You have cousins in your bones. Be careful how you talk about strangers — you are already made out of them."
 
---
 
*The children learned it. The children's children learned it. Somewhere along the way the wives lost their names, and the cold-stone people and the high-east people were folded into the word* nations, *and a scribe in a hot room wrote it all down in a list because a list is what survives a scribe.*
 
*And the list is still there. Shem, Ham, Japheth. Three stones in the pocket of the world, rattling, saying something.*
