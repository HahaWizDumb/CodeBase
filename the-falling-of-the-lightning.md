# The Falling of the Lightning

*Being a letter of Elias Weld, sometime minister of the gospel, to his brother Amos; written at St. Charles, in the State of Missouri, in the year of our Lord 1849.*

---

Brother —

You ask whether I hold yet to what our grandfather held. I will set down what I saw, and you may judge me, and God may judge us both.

## I. The inheritance

You remember his shelf. Strachey's *Historie of Travaile*, and the Captain's *Generall Historie*, and Whitaker's little book out of Virginia, *Good Newes*, wherein the minister at Henrico wrote that the Indians of the Powhatan served the devil, and named him, and the name was **Okeus**. A god that struck and did not bless. A god that had to be fed. Their priests, our grandfather said, were the devil's own curates; and their custom of taking the boys away into the woods, and giving them a drink that emptied them of their own names, and bringing them back as men who did not know their mothers — this, he said, was the devil's baptism, for the devil is a poor poet and can only rhyme.

I carried that sentence west with me like a coin under the tongue.

And I confess to you the smallness of the thing that first turned my head toward the upper river. It was a sound. The traders spoke of a ceremony among the Mandans called the **O-kee-pa**, and I heard *Okeus* in it, and my heart leapt up as a scholar's heart leaps at a false etymology. The tongues are not kin. Virginia is Algonquian and the Missouri is Siouan, and the likeness of those syllables is worth exactly nothing, as I learned afterward and as I set down here so that you shall not repeat my folly in company. But God is not above using a bad reason to move a stubborn man. I went.

## II. My thorn

You know already what I carry, but I must name it here because the whole letter turns upon it.

Since my twenty-sixth year there has been a hot wire behind the right eye. It comes without warning and stays three days, and in the worst of it the lamp is like broken glass thrown at my face, and I lie in the dark and am no use to God or man. Three times I prayed as the apostle prayed, and I used his very words, and I called it what he called it, *the messenger of Satan sent to buffet me*, and I asked that it might depart from me. And it did not depart.

Paul asked thrice. It was not taken away. For years I read that answer the way a man reads a bill he cannot pay.

## III. The village, 1832

Earth lodges on the bluff, round as loaves, and the great open plaza in the middle of them, and standing in the plaza an enclosure of planks set upright, which they called the Big Canoe, and which was the holiest thing in the town.

On the first morning a man came into the village out of the west, his body whitened, robed in wolfskins, carrying a pipe. He went from lodge to lodge and told at each door how the waters had once risen and covered the world, and how he alone had come through in a great canoe, and that the planks yonder in the plaza were the timber of it. And at each door he asked a gift, an edged tool, a knife or a hatchet, and these were gathered up and afterward given to the river, that the waters might not come again.

Brother, I stood with my hat in my two hands. I had ridden a thousand miles of grass to find the devil, and the first thing I found was Noah.

## IV. The lodge

I will not dwell where the eye wants to dwell. I have read Mr. Catlin's book since, and he lingers, and I think a man should not.

Four days the young men lay in the medicine lodge without food, without water, without sleep. On the fourth day they were taken up one by one. Splints were passed beneath the flesh of the breast and the shoulders, and cords made fast to them, and each young man was drawn up from the floor of the lodge by his own flesh, and weights were hung upon him — the skulls of buffalo, that his fathers had killed — and he was turned until sense left him and he hung as a dead man hangs. Then he was lowered, and lay untouched until he could crawl; and gave the little finger of his left hand upon a buffalo skull; and last of all was made to run about the Big Canoe, dragging the skulls behind him by the splints still in him, until the flesh gave way and let them go.

The first scripture that came to me was the one you would expect. *And they cried aloud, and cut themselves after their manner with knives and lancets, till the blood gushed out upon them.* I had it ready. I had brought it with me from Virginia in our grandfather's handwriting.

The second was *Shall I give my firstborn for my transgression, the fruit of my body for the sin of my soul?*

The third I did not want and it came anyway, and it has never left me since. *They pierced my hands and my feet.* *He was wounded for our transgressions.* *Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree.*

## V. The one who was driven out

Hear now the thing I cannot explain and will not pretend to.

On the fourth day there came into that village a figure painted black from the crown to the heel, with a mouth painted white, carrying a staff; and he was the Evil One, and they named him so. And the people gave back before him and he went where he liked and did as he liked and no man hindered him. And at the last the women of the village came upon him together, and one of them took the staff out of his hand and broke his power in the sight of everybody, and they drove him out of the town onto the open prairie, the boys running after him and pelting him with clods; and the staff was kept in the village for a token, and the women were honoured for it.

I stood in that crowd with my mouth full of the third chapter of Genesis. *I will put enmity between thee and the woman, and between thy seed and her seed; it shall bruise thy head.*

I had come to report to the societies that here the devil was enthroned and adored. And here instead were a people who once a year, at the ripening of the corn, put him out of the town by the hands of their women.

## VI. The question

There was a young man I had watched hang, and three days after, when he could sit up, I went to him with Beauchamp the interpreter, who was half French and wholly a liar in matters of trade but honest in this.

I asked him why.

He was a long time answering, and what came back through Beauchamp was this, or near it: that the horse was his father's gift, and the corn was the women's, and the powder and the kettle came from the traders and were not truly his. That his body was the one thing that was his own to give. That a man must give what is his own, or he has given nothing.

I wrote in my book that night, and I copy it out for you as I wrote it: *Lord, what have I ever given that was my own?*

## VII. The apostle

Consider Paul, whom I have preached these twenty years and never understood until the Missouri.

Of the Jews five times received he forty stripes save one. Thrice was he beaten with rods. Once was he stoned — at Lystra, and dragged out of the city, and left for dead in the ditch, and got up and walked back in. There was not a hand's breadth on that man from ankle to shoulder that was not written on. And he says of it: *I bear in my body the marks of the Lord Jesus.* The word is the word for the brand burnt into a slave, and what it says is: *I am owned.*

So he too gave his body. He is not on the other side of any line you could draw between him and that young man in the lodge.

And yet mark what he never once says. He never says his scars bought anything. He says the reverse of it, and says it hot: *Was Paul crucified for you?* When he says he fills up that which is behind of the afflictions of Christ, he is not a man paying down an unpaid debt; he is a man carrying water from a well that is already full and will not run dry for the pouring.

And the thorn was not taken. Three prayers, and no removal, and instead this: *My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness.*

Which is to say: I will not take the thing out of thy flesh. I will come and be in it with thee.

There is the difference, brother, and it is the only one, and it is not the difference our grandfather drew. It is not between people who bleed and people who do not. Every altar that was ever built has a groove cut in the stone for the blood to run down, ours as much as theirs; ask Abel, ask Aaron, ask the Passover door. The difference is between blood **offered** and blood **received**. Between the man who hangs in order to buy the year, and the Man who hung once and said *It is finished*, and the men who afterward carry marks in their bodies not as coin but as a signature.

## VIII. The tree

Some seven years on, west of the Platte among the Sioux, I saw the other form of it, and it was worse for me because it was nearer.

Not a lodge but a tree. A cottonwood felled with ceremony and set upright in the ground and dressed with cloth and offerings; and men tethered to it by the flesh of the breast, and leaning back against the cords with their whole weight, and dancing so, with their faces to the sun, and blowing on whistles of bone, until the flesh tore through and let them go free of it.

A man's entire weight hung upon a piece of standing wood by the flesh of him.

*Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree.* I could not stop the verse. It came around and around like the men came around, and the more I tried to make it condemn what I was looking at, the more plainly it did what Paul made it do — it turned and pointed east, over my shoulder, at a hill outside a city wall, where the curse was taken up by One who did not need to be tethered because they had used nails.

## IX. The revelation of the devil

Now I will tell you where our grandfather went wrong, and I with him for thirty years.

The Lord was asked about Satan exactly once in circumstances like these. The seventy came back glad, saying that even the devils were subject unto them through His name. And He did not answer, *Yes, and yonder lie his provinces, in Virginia and upon the Missouri, and you shall go and find him sitting in them.*

He said: ***I beheld Satan as lightning fall from heaven.***

*Fall.* It is a thing already down. It is the report of a battle over, given to men who thought they were opening one. And He said it again on the eve of His own hanging: *Now is the judgment of this world: now shall the prince of this world be cast out* — and the hour He appointed for that casting out was the hour they lifted Him up on the wood.

If that is true, then the devil is not enthroned in the medicine lodge, because the devil is not enthroned anywhere. He is a fallen thing running out across the prairie with the boys throwing clods after him and a woman holding his broken staff.

And what I watched in that lodge was not his liturgy. It was the oldest sentence the human race has, said in the only language any people has ever had for saying it: *I own nothing but this body; take it, and let the people live.* God did not answer that sentence by forbidding it to be spoken. He answered it by speaking it Himself, in flesh, once, and forever, so that it need never again be paid for out of anybody's chest.

## X. The judgment turned round

One thing more, and it is the thing I would rather leave out.

Whitaker called their god the devil. We have all called it. And five years after I stood in that plaza, the smallpox came up the river in the hold of a steamboat, and it took the Mandan nation almost entire; of a people I had seen numbered in their thousands there were left, they say, something near a hundred and fifty souls, and those went to live among the Hidatsa, and the villages on the bluff stood empty with the Big Canoe still standing in the middle of them.

We came with the name of the devil in our mouths for them and the plague in our cargo.

*Therefore thou art inexcusable, O man, whosoever thou art that judgest: for wherein thou judgest another, thou condemnest thyself; for thou that judgest doest the same things.*

I do not write to tell you that all altars are one altar. I do not believe it and I will not say it. I hold what I have always held, that there is one Name under heaven given among men. But I will never again say that a man who gives away the only thing he owns is serving the devil, when the devil's entire trade is to persuade men that they owe nothing and are owed everything.

## XI. Sufficient

The right eye is dark now and the left is going. I asked a fourth time, two winters ago, being weak and ashamed of it. The answer was what it was the third time. It is a strange comfort, Amos, to be told *no* in a voice you would know anywhere.

I bear no marks. I have been beaten by nothing worse than a headache. There is no scar on me that would prove anything to anybody. And I think that is the trial appointed for men of our sort, who have a great deal to say and nothing whatever to bring: to learn to read that one word, and to stop at it, and add nothing.

*Sufficient.*

Not the finger on the skull. Not the strip of my own skin. Not even our grandfather's good opinion.

Yours in the hope of the resurrection,

**Elias Weld**

## Note on sources

*Elias Weld, his brother, Beauchamp, and the whole letter are invented. The materials underneath it are not, and here is the line between them.*

**Documented.**
- **Okeus / Okee** — a principal deity of the Powhatan of Tidewater Virginia, described by John Smith, William Strachey, and others as demanding propitiation. Alexander Whitaker's *Good Newes from Virginia* (1613) is a real tract that identifies Powhatan religion with devil-worship. The **huskanaw**, an initiation in which boys were secluded, given an intoxicant (*wysoccan*), and returned as men who ritually did not know their former life, is likewise attested.
- **The O-kee-pa** — the Mandan four-day ceremony on the Upper Missouri, recorded by George Catlin (1832; published at length in *O-kee-pa*, 1867) and independently by Prince Maximilian of Wied. The flood narrative, the figure who arrives from the west and collects edged tools sacrificed to the water, the plank enclosure ("Big Canoe"), the fasting, the suspension by skewers with buffalo skulls as weights, the finger sacrifice, and the concluding "last race" are all in the ethnographic record.
- **O-kee-hee-de**, the black-painted Evil Spirit who enters the village on the fourth day and is finally disarmed and driven out by the women, is also in Catlin. (Catlin's fuller account of this figure's obscene role was disbelieved in his lifetime and was later corroborated; I have kept the episode restrained.)
- **The Sun Dance** with a consecrated cottonwood pole and piercing to the tree is a distinct Plains tradition — not the O-kee-pa — which is why the narrator meets it in a separate place and year. It is still practiced today.
- **The 1837 smallpox epidemic** at Fort Clark, arriving on the steamboat *St. Peter's*, effectively destroyed the Mandan as an independent nation; survivor counts in the 100–150 range are the usual estimates.

**Coincidental, and flagged inside the story on purpose.** *Okeus* (Eastern Algonquian) and *O-kee-pa* (Siouan) are unrelated words from unrelated language families. The resemblance is noise. The narrator's excitement at it is meant as a small confession of how easily comparative religion turns into paretymology.

**Interpretation, not history.** Every theological move here — the Genesis 3:15 reading of the driving-out, the "blood offered vs. blood received" hinge, the use of Luke 10:18 as a *past-tense* report, the turn of Romans 2:1 back on the missionary — belongs to the fictional narrator. Reading Genesis into someone else's ceremony is exactly the habit the letter is trying to catch itself doing, and the ending is meant to leave the narrator convicted rather than vindicated.

**Scripture used** (KJV): 1 Kings 18:28; Micah 6:7; Psalm 22:16; Isaiah 53:5; Genesis 3:15; Deuteronomy 21:23 with Galatians 3:13; 2 Corinthians 11:24–25; Acts 14:19; Galatians 6:17; Colossians 1:24; 1 Corinthians 1:13; 2 Corinthians 12:7–9; Luke 10:18; John 12:31; Romans 2:1; John 19:30; Acts 4:12.
